DV2008 · Individual case study · Goodreads redesign

Narrow to Now

Most of us have a Want to Read list we never actually read from. This is how our team turned a 40-book Goodreads list into three picks for tonight, told through the screens I designed and tested.

Role
Research, ideation, testing, and the picks + results screens
Team
Group 5 Beng-Beng: Janice, Michelle, Michael and me
Timeline
Sep to Nov 2026
Tools
Figma (UI, prototyping, variables), FigJam

01 Overview

Saving a book is easy. Picking one isn’t.

01

Context

Goodreads is where readers keep track of what they want to read, but it’s rarely where they find it. In our A1 survey of 41 university readers, 28 found books through friends and family and 28 through TikTok or Instagram. Six found them on Goodreads.

02

Problem

Books get saved in five different places and the lists keep growing. P3 said it during testing: she remembered saving the books, “but not the reason”. So the hard part comes right at the end, when you have to choose one.

03

Objectives

  • Get someone from a 40-book list to one book they actually start.
  • Keep the reason a book was saved, so it still makes sense months later.
  • Leave the final call with the reader. We suggest, they pick.

02 My contribution

What I did, and what we did together

Me

What I owned

  1. A1I walked through Goodreads’ discover → search → evaluate → decide → save journey, wrote our interview guide, ran interviews, and wrote Finding 3: saving books doesn’t help anyone choose.
  2. A2I sketched ideas A, B and C, ran concept tests and pulled the A vs E notes together. In the prototype I designed the questions and the picks screen, and I presented slides 1 to 5.
  3. A3I designed the recommendation cards and results (screens 08, 08b, 08c, 11 and E2): Shuffle, Change answers, no shuffles left, no matches, only two matches. I facilitated P1, took notes for P2, and turned all five sessions into numbers against our A2 metrics.

Team

Team effort

  1. A1Janice built the survey and wrote Finding 1. Michelle planned the research and wrote Finding 2. Michael set up the affinity map and the problem statement. We sorted every note together.
  2. A2Twelve ideas, three each. Janice wrote the HMWs and decision matrix, Michelle ran scoring and the impact/effort chart, Michael did the competitor scan and user flow. Choosing Narrow to Now was a group call.
  3. A3One feature area each: Michelle the saving flow, Michael the list and questions, Janice the book pages and actions. P5, synthesis and the fixes happened in group meetings.

One problem I owned

In concept testing, one reader couldn’t name a mood and another just wanted to skip that question. So on my questions and picks screen, every question got an “Any” option and the picks got a “Change answers” link. Nobody should be stuck with the first three books.

03 Process

From a research finding to one direction

My A1 walkthrough · the Goodreads journey today

  1. 01DiscoverTikTok, friends, Instagram. Mostly off Goodreads.
  2. 02SearchLook the title up to see what it actually is.
  3. 03EvaluateStar average, blurb, a few reviews.
  4. 04DecideNo way to compare books side by side, or to remember why one was saved.Finding 3: people get stuck here
  5. 05SaveTap Want to Read. The list quietly passes 40 books.

Goodreads handles saving well. Deciding is the step it leaves to you, and that’s the step we designed for.

01

Research insight

People find books elsewhere and save them everywhere. My Finding 3 came out of the journey walkthrough: Goodreads helps you save, then leaves you on your own at “decide”.

02

Ideation, A to L

Twelve ideas, three each. Mine were A (a mood-based shortlist), B (save from anywhere) and C (remember why I saved this).

03

Concept testing

Idea A against idea E (“This or That”) with 5 readers. 3 picked A, 1 picked E, and 1 said it depends on the day.

04

Chosen direction

Narrow to Now. Save a book with a reason (C), narrow to three picks (A), then check each one with an “Is it for me?” card (K).

04 Design decisions

Three calls that shaped the solution

Decision 01

Go with A, not E

Decision
Build the “What should I read now?” flow.
Why
It gets to a shortlist in fewer steps, and suits someone who wants to start reading soon.
Evidence
3 of 5 testers chose A. One said E is for browsing and A is for “picking for this week”.

Decision 02

Add “Any” and “Change answers”

Decision
Every question gets an “Any” option, and picks can be redone.
Why
Some readers can’t name a mood. Others only care about length.
Evidence
One tester couldn’t answer the mood question. Another wanted to skip it.

Decision 03

Merge C, A and K into one flow

Decision
Saving with a reason, a three-book shortlist and a judging card become one flow.
Why
Without the saved reason, three picks are just three random titles. K makes them easier to weigh.
Evidence
A scored highest. K got full marks too, but only made sense as the step after A.

05 Refining in A3

What changed after the A2 feedback

Our lecturer liked the link to research, then asked three hard questions. Would people really add a reason? What happens if they don’t? Do the three features feel like one product? We kept the direction and split the prototype into four feature areas. Mine was the picks and results, so Change 02 was on me.

Change 01 · Michelle

Save first, reason after

Before. Want to Read asked why before it saved anything. It felt like homework.

After. One tap saves the book. The reason is optional, and Skip is as easy as Add.

Change 02 · my screens

Picks work with no reasons

Before. My picks leaned on saved reasons, so a book with no reason made a weak pick.

After. Picks match on mood, pace and length tags. With no reason, the card says “No reason yet” and shows a small Add link instead of making one up.

Change 03 · Janice, Michael

One flow with a real ending

Before. Saving, picking and judging felt like three features, and a pick simply stopped.

After. One “Saved because” label follows the book everywhere, and every pick ends in Start reading, Not now or Remove.

Craft 01

Answers that carry through

Mood, time and length live in Figma variables. Choose “Cosy” and my results title and “Based on” chips say cosy too.

I also wired the branches. “Long (450+)” lands on my no-match state and “Dark” gives only two picks, so testers could actually hit the edge cases.

Craft 02

Fitting into the team’s kit

We all worked from Goodreads’ own type (Merriweather and Lato), its cream and brown, and one set of components. My cards reuse the cover, label and button, so they sit next to my teammates’ screens without looking stitched on.

We also kept a copy of today’s Want to Read page so we could test old against new.

Craft 03

Accessible by default

Goodreads’ green button is 3.4:1 on white, under the 4.5:1 minimum. As a team we moved main buttons to their darker green (5.2:1).

Shuffle and Change answers on my cards are 44 px tall, and nothing relies on colour alone.

07 Testing

What we tested, and what happened

Narrow to Now takes a 40+ book list down to three that fit tonight. The reader still makes the final pick. Saving, choosing and judging run as one loop that ends with a book on Currently Reading.

We tested with five readers aged 20 to 23, from two who had never opened Goodreads to one who reads 35 books a year. Each picked a cosy, short book twice: first on Goodreads today, then on Narrow to Now. I facilitated P1, took notes for P2, and turned all five sessions into numbers.

What we saw

  • 3 of 5 added a reason when saving. The 2 who skipped went back to check the book had saved at all.
  • 2 of 5 thought Shuffle and Change answers did the same thing. Both buttons live on my results screen.
  • 4 of 5 read the mood tags and the “Is it for me?” trade-offs before choosing. P4 didn’t, because they already knew the book.

Five people is a small group, so I report counts, not percentages.

08 Reflection

Looking back

01

What worked

Testing A against E early made the direction easy to defend. And because I built the no-match and two-match states into my results, the A2 feedback had real answers on screen.

02

What didn’t

My A2 picks screen only worked if people had added a reason. I was designing for the reader who fills everything in. Then the feedback came, and 2 of 5 testers skipped the reason anyway. Honestly, I was annoyed with myself for not seeing it sooner.

03

What I’d improve

P2 said “Calling it Show 3 more picks would be easier,” and P4 assumed both buttons changed the selection. Next round I’d rename them “Show 3 more picks” and “Change my preferences”, then retest. I’d also ask Michelle to show the saved message before the reason step.

04

How I grew

In A2 I linked static screens. By A3 my results reacted to answers and branched into edge states. Running P1 myself taught me that a test I can compare, with confidence and ease scores, tells me more than a pile of opinions.
